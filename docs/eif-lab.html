Efficient influence functions in a hybrid trial

What happens to a treatment contrast when an external control cohort becomes more variable, even if its mean outcome stays the same? Follow that change from a borrowing rule to its efficient influence function (EIF) and its sampling uncertainty.

Borrowing and the gap

Consider an iid mixture of trial and external records. Each record has a binary covariate X. Trial participants (S = 1) receive treatment (A = 1) with known probability ⅔ or concurrent control with probability ⅓. External participants (S = 0) all receive control. Treatment adds one unit to the trial control mean in both strata.

The precision rule assigns internal controls a share proportional to their availability divided by their variance. At the starting settings, the external share is ¾. A control discrepancy of 0.20 then shifts the borrowing target by 0.15 outcome units. Increase the external variance to see why a less precise external cohort has a smaller effect on the contrast.

See the data law and target formulas

At the central law, Pr(X = 1) = ½. Set e = ⅔ and q = Pr(S = 1). Conditional means are m1,0(x) = 0.4x for internal controls, m1(x) = 0.4x + 1 for treated participants, and m0(x) = 0.4x − δ(x) for external controls. The two trial variances are one. For each group g, generate Y = mg(x) + √vg(x) Z, with Z equal to −√3, 0, or √3 with probabilities ⅙, ⅔, and ⅙. Exact sums over these 18 possible records give every expectation below.

πI(x) = p(x)(1 − e) / v1,0(x),   πE(x) = [1 − p(x)] / v0(x)

λ(x) = πI(x) / [πI(x) + πE(x)]

The transported causal estimand (TCE) θ̃ averages m1(X) − λ(X)m1,0(X) − [1 − λ(X)]m0(X) over trial covariates. The trial effect θ averages m1(X) − m1,0(X) over the same distribution. Their difference averages [1 − λ(X)]δ(X) over trial covariates.

Check the derivative along a path

A path changes one part of the data law by a small amount ε. Its score ℓ(O) is the derivative of the record’s log probability at ε = 0. For a target T, an influence function φ has mean zero and satisfies dT/dε = E[φ(O)ℓ(O)] for every allowed path. Select a target and compare this prediction to a direct recalculation.

What makes an influence function efficient?

With known randomization probability e, the assignment residual U = S(A − e) is orthogonal to every allowed score. Adding cU to the EIF therefore preserves the pathwise derivatives. Move c to see how two influence functions for the same target can imply different precision.

Compare restrictions at a common law

If the internal and external control means agree at each x, the TCE and trial effect have the same value. An efficiency bound also depends on which departures the model allows.

Connect the examples to the manuscript

Section 4 and Table 1 of the paper derive the unrestricted HCT-TCE EIF and its terms. The section on the efficiency bound works through the variance perturbation. The design-specific corollaries describe the common-control-mean model. Supporting Information Section S4 provides the proofs. The discrete outcome law here has the same first four standardized moments as a Gaussian law, so the starting example gives the paper’s per-record TCE variance bound of 4.528125.

For background on scores, tangent spaces and canonical gradients, see Kennedy, “Semiparametric theory”. For external-control borrowing under a common conditional mean, see Li et al., “Improving Efficiency of Inference in Clinical Trials with External Control Data”.
